# Chapter 1: Stop, Jump, Stop

Right now, as you read this sentence, your eyes are doing something you would probably swear they are not. It feels as if your gaze is sliding smoothly along the line, the way a finger might trace the words. It isn’t. Your eyes are hopping: landing on a word, pausing, leaping ahead, landing again, several times every second. The smooth glide is a story your brain tells you afterward, and it tells it so well that almost nobody notices.

This pocket guide is about that story and the clever machinery hiding behind it. It is also, quietly, a user’s manual for the app you are reading it in, because Gaze Reader depends on exactly these habits of the reading eye. But you don’t need to care about software to enjoy what follows. You only need a pair of eyes, and you’re already using those.

## The rhythm of reading

Reading researchers divide eye movements into two kinds of event. A *fixation* is a pause: a brief moment when the eyes hold relatively still and take in visual information. A *saccade* is the jump from one fixation to the next. The word comes from French, where it means something like a jerk or a sudden tug, and it suits the movement well. Saccades are among the fastest movements the human body can make.

The numbers are worth savoring. When skilled adults read English silently, a typical fixation lasts roughly a quarter of a second, with averages usually falling somewhere around 200 to 250 milliseconds. Individual fixations vary a great deal, from less than a tenth of a second on an easy little word to half a second or more on a difficult one. The saccades in between are far briefer, often only about 20 to 40 milliseconds. Put together, that makes a rhythm of roughly three to four fixations per second. In an hour of reading, your eyes can easily make more than ten thousand jumps.

A forward saccade in English reading carries the eyes about seven to nine letters on average. Researchers like to measure these jumps in letters rather than in millimeters or degrees, and for a lovely reason. Hold a book closer and the letters grow larger on your retina; hold it farther away and they shrink. The saccades grow and shrink right along with them, so that, measured in letters, the jump stays about the same. The reading eye, it seems, is tuned to text rather than to rulers.

Even a fixation is not perfectly still. During each pause the eyes drift and tremble by tiny amounts, and now and then they make a miniature jump called a *microsaccade*. These motions are much too small to feel, and they are thought to help keep the image on the retina from fading, since the visual system responds to change and tends to tune out anything that stays perfectly constant.

Historically speaking, people have known about the jumps for a surprisingly short time. In the late nineteenth century, the French ophthalmologist Émile Javal and his colleagues observed that reading proceeds in a series of quick movements and pauses rather than a smooth sweep. Some of the earliest devices for recording eye movements were startlingly invasive, involving a tiny cup resting directly on the eye. Modern eye trackers, thankfully, use cameras.

## Why not glide?

If saccades are so fast, why bother with them at all? Why not glide along the line at a steady speed, like a scanner?

The answer starts at the back of your eye. Sharp, detailed vision comes from a small central patch of the retina called the *fovea*, packed with light-sensitive cone cells. The fovea covers only about two degrees of your visual field, roughly the width of your thumbnail held at arm’s length. At a normal reading distance, that is enough to see perhaps six to eight letters clearly. Outside it, sharpness drops off steeply. In the *parafovea*, which extends to about five degrees on either side, you can still make out word shapes and some letters. Beyond that, in the periphery, words dissolve into gray smudges.

You can check this for yourself. Pick a single word in the middle of this line and hold your eyes on it. Without moving them, try to read the words at the far ends of the line. You’ll be able to tell that words are there, but probably not which words they are.

So reading requires bringing each new stretch of text onto the fovea, and the quickest way to do that is to jump. There is a second reason, too. The smooth kind of eye movement you use to follow a bird across the sky, called *smooth pursuit*, needs something moving to follow. Without a moving target, almost no one can glide their eyes smoothly on purpose. Ask a friend to watch your eyes while you try to slide your gaze slowly along the top edge of a wall: they will see a series of little hops. Now trace the same edge slowly with a fingertip and follow your finger, and your friend will see your eyes glide. A line of text holds perfectly still, so your eyes do what they always do with still things. They jump.

Why don’t you notice all that jumping? During a saccade, the brain turns down its sensitivity to visual input, a phenomenon called *saccadic suppression*, and the blurry smear that would otherwise arrive is masked by the crisp image at the next fixation. Try this in front of a mirror: look at your left eye, then your right eye, back and forth. You will never see your own eyes move, although a friend standing beside you will see them move quite clearly. In ordinary reading, something like a tenth of your time is spent mid-jump, effectively not seeing, and you never feel the gaps.

It helps, then, to think of the reading eye less as a scanner and more as a photographer, taking three or four quick snapshots a second and choosing where to aim each one with remarkable skill. The rest of this book is about how it chooses.

# Chapter 2: The Window You Read Through

If the fovea is so small, how much of the page can you actually use during a single fixation? It certainly feels as if you can see the whole line at once. But what we feel we see is a poor guide to what we actually see, and for a long time the question was surprisingly hard to answer.

The answer came from an ingenious method developed in the 1970s by the reading researchers George McConkie and Keith Rayner, called the *moving window*. A reader sits at a computer that tracks their eyes very precisely. Wherever they look, the text within a small window around that point appears normally, while everything outside the window is replaced, for example with rows of x’s. Each time the eyes jump, the computer redraws the display during the saccade itself, so the window travels with the reader’s gaze. Because vision is suppressed during saccades, readers generally don’t see the changes happen.

Researchers can then shrink or widen the window and watch what happens. When the window is small, reading slows down and feels effortful. As it grows, reading speeds up, until at some point making it any larger stops helping. That point marks the *perceptual span*: the region from which a reader actually gets useful information during a fixation. Remarkably, once the window is at least that large, many readers have no idea that anything unusual is going on, even though most of the screen is filled with gibberish.

## A lopsided window

For skilled readers of English, the perceptual span turns out to be surprisingly small and strikingly lopsided. It extends only about three or four letters to the left of the point of fixation but roughly fourteen or fifteen letters to the right. Your attention, in other words, leans forward, toward the text you are about to read rather than the text you have just read.

The lopsidedness is not built into the eye. It follows the direction of reading. Readers of Hebrew, which runs from right to left, show a span that extends farther to the *left*. And in a lovely twist, people who read both Hebrew and English have been found to shift the asymmetry depending on which language is in front of them. The window is shaped by attention and habit, not by anatomy.

The span also depends on the writing system. A Chinese character packs much more information into a single symbol than an English letter does, and readers of Chinese have a perceptual span of only a few characters to the right of fixation.

Not everything inside the window is used equally, either. Readers can actually identify words only within a smaller region, extending about seven or eight letters to the right of fixation. Farther out, they pick up coarser information: where the spaces fall, how long the next word is, where a long word begins. That rough sketch of what lies ahead is exactly what the eyes need to plan their next jump.

## Reading before you arrive

Here is one of the most striking discoveries in the whole field: you begin processing a word before you look at it directly.

Researchers showed this with another clever display change, sometimes called the *boundary technique*. An invisible boundary is placed just before a target word. Until the reader’s eyes cross the boundary, the target location shows a preview, which might be the real word or something different. The moment the eyes cross, mid-saccade, the real word appears. Readers almost never notice the swap. But when the preview is accurate, the fixation on the word is shorter, typically by a few tens of milliseconds, than when it is not. Part of the work of reading each word gets done during the previous fixation, while the word is still out in the parafovea.

The eyes are also particular about where they land. They tend to land a little to the left of a word’s center, a spot researchers call the *preferred viewing location*. The eyes seem to aim for the middle of the word and, more often than not, fall a little short. That is close to ideal anyway, because a word is generally recognized fastest when the eyes land near its middle.

Spaces between words help enormously with all of this. They mark where words begin and end, which lets the eyes aim their jumps. Many ancient Greek and Latin texts were written without spaces, a style called *scriptio continua*, and modern experiments that remove the spaces from English text find that reading slows down considerably. The humble space may be one of the most important inventions in the history of reading.

So as you read, you look through a narrow, forward-leaning window that travels along each line in quick hops, previewing what comes next before you arrive. You never see the whole page sharply at once. It just feels as if you do.

# Chapter 3: Glancing Back, Sweeping Down

Not every saccade moves forward. In skilled readers, something like ten to fifteen percent of saccades go backward, against the direction of reading. These backward jumps are called *regressions*, which is an unfortunate name for a perfectly sensible habit.

Most regressions are short. The eyes hop back a few letters within a word, or to the word just before. Often this happens because a jump overshot its target, or because a word wasn’t fully recognized before the eyes moved on. Longer regressions are more interesting. They tend to happen when a sentence stops making sense.

## When a sentence misleads you

Consider this sentence:

> While the chef stirred the soup boiled over.

Most people read “the chef stirred the soup” as a neat little unit, and then stumble on “boiled,” which suddenly seems to have no subject. The sentence is perfectly grammatical once you mentally add a comma after “stirred,” but you have been led up the wrong path. Linguists call sentences like this *garden-path sentences*, and they have collected a whole greenhouse of them.

Eye trackers show exactly what happens when a reader is led astray. Fixations on the troublesome word lengthen, and the eyes become much more likely to jump back toward the earlier part of the sentence to sort it out. When researchers have made re-reading impossible, by masking each word once the eyes have passed it, readers’ understanding of tricky sentences suffered. Regressions, it turns out, are not a failure of reading. They are one of its repair tools.

The same pattern shows up whenever anyone reads something difficult. As text gets harder, fixations get longer, forward saccades get shorter, and regressions become more frequent. Children who are just learning to read show all three: more fixations, longer fixations and more regressions, with a smaller perceptual span that widens as their skill grows. Many adults who struggle with reading, including many people with dyslexia, show similar patterns. The mainstream scientific view is that these eye movement patterns mostly *reflect* difficulty with processing language rather than cause it, which is why eye exercises alone are not considered an effective treatment for reading difficulties.

## The return sweep

Then there is the grandest routine movement in reading: the *return sweep*. When your eyes reach the end of a line, they make one long leftward saccade to the beginning of the next line. In a typical book, that single jump can cover most of the width of the text column, several times the length of an ordinary forward saccade.

Your eyes rarely visit the very edges of a line. The first fixation on a line usually lands several letters in from the left margin, and the last one usually falls several letters short of the right end; the parafovea picks up the rest. Return sweeps also tend to fall a little short of their target, landing a few letters into the new line, and are often followed by a small corrective saccade back toward the start. The first fixation on a new line tends to last a bit longer than average, as though the eyes are getting their bearings, while the last fixation on a line tends to be a bit shorter.

Line length matters here. When lines are very long, the return sweep becomes harder to aim, and readers are more likely to land on the wrong line, either re-reading the one they just finished or skipping one entirely. When lines are very short, readers make return sweeps constantly, which can make reading feel choppy. Typographers have a long-standing rule of thumb that comfortable lines hold somewhere around forty-five to seventy-five characters. That’s one reason Gaze Reader lets you adjust the width of the text column.

## The heartbeat the app listens for

The return sweep has a special place in this book, because it is the heartbeat Gaze Reader listens for. As you’ll see in a later chapter, a webcam is far better at telling where you’re looking from side to side than up and down. But the return sweep is a big, unmistakable leftward movement, easy to spot even in a noisy signal, and each one means “next line.” By following the steady left-to-right march of fixations and counting the sweeps, the app can keep track of which line you’re on far more reliably than it could from the vertical signal alone.

At the bottom of a page, something charming happens. Your eyes finish the last line and make their usual sweep to the left, looking for the next line. It isn’t there. That little moment of searching is one of the clearest signs that you’re ready for more, and it is one of the cues the app uses to know that it’s time to turn the page.

# Chapter 4: The Words You Never Look At

Here is something that surprises almost everyone: in ordinary skilled reading, the eyes skip a large share of the words entirely. Somewhere around a quarter to a third of the words on a page never receive a fixation of their own. And yet you read them.

## Which words get skipped

Skipping is far from random. The strongest influence is word length. Two- and three-letter words are skipped much of the time, often more often than they are looked at, while words of eight letters or more are almost always fixated and sometimes fixated twice. Short, common function words such as *the*, *of* and *and* are among the most frequently skipped of all.

Skipped words are not ignored. They are usually identified from the parafovea during the fixation before the jump, which is possible because short, familiar words can be recognized from slightly outside the sharpest part of vision. The eyes don’t need to land on *the* to know it is there.

Frequency matters too, even for the words the eyes do land on. Common words get shorter fixations than rare words of the same length. A rare word such as *tessellate* tends to hold the eyes longer than a common word of equal length such as *understand*. Every reader carries a mental dictionary, and the words used most often are the quickest to look up.

## Predictability

The other big influence is predictability. Researchers measure it with a simple game called the *cloze task*: show people a sentence up to a certain word and ask them to guess what comes next. If nearly everyone guesses correctly, the word is highly predictable.

Take the sentence *The children built a snowman and gave it a carrot for a nose.* By the time you reach the last word, “nose” is almost inevitable. Now imagine the same sentence ending in “hat.” Eye-tracking studies consistently find that highly predictable words are skipped more often and, when they are fixated, receive shorter fixations than unpredictable ones. The mind is always running a little ahead of the eyes, making reasonable guesses about what is coming, and when a glimpse from the parafovea confirms a guess, the eyes can move on.

This forward-leaning guesswork helps explain a classic perceptual trick. Read the following sentence once, at normal speed:

> I love Paris in the the springtime.

Many people read it more than once before noticing the doubled word. Skipping and expectation are likely part of the reason: a short, predictable word such as *the* is exactly the kind of word the eyes jump over and the mind fills in.

## A famous myth

You may have seen an internet meme claiming that you can easily read words with jumbled letters, as long as the first and last letters stay in place, and that researchers at a famous university proved it. The meme’s story about where the finding came from doesn’t hold up, and the effect itself is much weaker than it claims. Jumbled text *can* often be read, especially when the words are short, the sentence is predictable and the swapped letters sit close together. But careful experiments find that transposed letters do slow readers down, and the slowdown is noticeably larger when the beginnings of words are disturbed. The first letters of a word turn out to matter a great deal.

## Where the eyes get their timing

All of this points to a close partnership between the eyes and the mind. Researchers sometimes call it the *eye-mind link*: the eyes tend to stay on a word until the brain has made enough progress with it, and then move on, with only a short lag between thinking and moving. That is why fixation durations respond so sensitively to how common, predictable or difficult a word is.

Detailed computer models of reading, such as the well-known *E-Z Reader* and *SWIFT*, try to capture this dance between attention and the eyes, simulating where real readers fixate, how long they pause and which words they skip. They still disagree about some basics, such as whether attention moves strictly one word at a time or spreads across several words at once, and testing their predictions has driven a great deal of research.

So the next time you finish a paragraph, remember that you didn’t look at every word in it. You didn’t need to. Your eyes and your mind were working as a team, and they are very good at it.

# Chapter 5: Fast, Slow and Just Right

How fast do people read? Most skilled adult readers of English read silently at somewhere around 200 to 300 words per minute. A large review of many studies put the average for adults reading non-fiction at a little under 250 words per minute, with fiction read a bit faster. Reading aloud is slower, usually under 200 words per minute. And every reader’s speed changes constantly with the difficulty of the text, the purpose of reading and how tired they are.

## What sets the pace

It’s tempting to think the eyes are the bottleneck, and that if only they moved faster, you would read faster. The evidence points elsewhere.

One remarkable line of experiments makes the point. In studies of *disappearing text*, each word vanishes from the screen shortly after the eyes land on it, in some conditions after only about fifty or sixty milliseconds. You might expect reading to fall apart. It doesn’t. People read these vanishing sentences fairly normally, and their eyes still linger at each word’s now-empty location for about as long as that word needs, with rare words still getting longer pauses than common ones. The eyes, it seems, collect the visual information they need very quickly. Most of each fixation is spent on something else: recognizing the word, fitting it into the sentence and planning the next jump.

So the real pace-setter is language processing. When text is easy, the eyes move briskly. When it gets harder, fixations lengthen, saccades shorten and regressions multiply, not because the eyes have become clumsy but because the mind needs more time.

## The trouble with speed reading

This is why claims of reading thousands of words per minute with full comprehension deserve a raised eyebrow. In 2016, a team of reading researchers published a thorough review of the evidence on speed reading. Their conclusion, broadly, was that there is a genuine trade-off between speed and accuracy. You can read faster, but past a certain point you understand and remember less, especially the details.

Studies comparing trained speed readers with ordinary readers have found that speed readers can do reasonably well at getting the general gist, particularly when they already know something about the topic, but they tend to miss details that ordinary readers catch. That is a perfectly useful skill, but it is closer to skilled skimming than to reading faster in the ordinary sense.

Some popular speed-reading tips also run into trouble with what we know. Training yourself to take in a whole line at a glance runs up against the limits of the fovea and the perceptual span. Stamping out regressions removes one of reading’s repair tools. And trying to eliminate the quiet inner voice many people hear while reading, sometimes called *subvocalization*, may backfire: evidence suggests that inner speech supports comprehension, particularly of complex sentences, and that suppressing it tends to hurt understanding of harder text.

What about apps that flash words one at a time in the center of the screen? This technique, called *rapid serial visual presentation*, removes eye movements entirely, which sounds efficient. But it also removes your preview of upcoming words and your ability to glance back, and it forces the same pace on words that deserve different amounts of time. At high speeds, comprehension tends to suffer.

## Skimming, wandering and choosing your speed

None of this makes skimming bad. When you want the gist of an article, or you are hunting for one particular fact, skimming is exactly the right tool. Good skimmers lean on headings, first sentences and key words. The trick is simply to know what you are trading: detail for speed.

Reading can also go wrong in a quieter way. Most of us have reached the bottom of a page and realized we have no idea what was on it. Researchers have caught this *mind-wandering* in the act with eye trackers: the eyes keep moving along the lines, but the length of each pause becomes less connected to how difficult each word is. The eyes are going through the motions while the mind is elsewhere. Noticing that it has happened and going back is not a failure. It is a skill.

If you would like to read faster in a way that lasts, the most reliable method is also the most pleasant: read a lot. The more words you know well and the more you know about a subject, the faster you can recognize words and connect ideas, and that is where reading speed actually comes from.

In the end, the best reading speed is the one at which you understand what you came for. Gaze Reader is built around that idea. It doesn’t push you to go faster. It waits for your eyes, however quickly or slowly they happen to be moving today.

# Chapter 6: How a Webcam Watches You Read

The eye trackers used in reading laboratories are impressive machines. Most shine invisible infrared light toward the eye and use a specialized camera to follow two things: the dark circle of the pupil and a tiny bright reflection of that light on the surface of the cornea, often called the *glint*. As the eye rotates, the pupil shifts relative to the glint, and after a careful calibration the tracker can convert that relationship into a gaze position. The best of these systems take hundreds or even a thousand or more measurements per second, and under good conditions they are accurate to within about half a degree. Many experiments also use a chin rest to keep the head still.

A laptop webcam has none of those advantages. It works in ordinary visible light, usually captures around thirty frames per second, and sees your whole face from an arm’s length away, so each eye may be only a few dozen pixels wide in the image. There is no dependable glint to measure against. So webcam eye tracking takes a different approach.

## From face to gaze

First, software finds your face in each video frame and fits a detailed map of landmarks to it. Gaze Reader uses a face-landmark model called MediaPipe Face Landmarker, which places a mesh of nearly five hundred points over the face, including points tracing each eyelid and the edge of each iris. It also estimates the pose of your head: how it is turned, tilted and positioned relative to the camera.

Next, the app turns those points into measurements: where each iris sits between the corners of the eye and between the lids, how open the eyes are, and which way the head is pointing. Where you look depends both on the direction of your eyes within your head and on the direction of your head itself, so both matter.

Then comes *calibration*. You look at a series of dots at known positions on the screen while the app records your eye and head measurements for each one. From these examples it learns a mapping from measurements to screen positions, using a form of regression designed to resist overfitting to the quirks of any single moment. After that, it can estimate where you’re looking from the measurements alone.

Finally, because frame-to-frame estimates are jittery, Gaze Reader passes them through a small adaptive filter with the charming name *One Euro filter*. It smooths heavily when your gaze is holding steady and lightly when it moves quickly, which calms the jitter without making the estimate lag behind your saccades.

Everything described here runs locally, inside your browser. The video from your camera never leaves your device. The only thing the eye tracker downloads is the face-landmark model itself.

## Why up and down is harder

Webcam gaze estimates are usually much better from side to side than up and down, for several reasons.

The opening of the eye is roughly three times as wide as it is tall. From side to side, the iris has a stretch of white to travel across, and the camera can watch it go; above and below, the lids already cover its edges. Screens are wider than they are tall, too, so looking from one side of a screen to the other turns your eyes through a bigger angle, and moves the iris by more pixels, than looking from top to bottom.

Vertical movements are also partly hidden: when you look down, your upper eyelid follows your eye downward and covers part of the iris, and eyelashes and glasses frames get in the way. The webcam usually sits at the top edge of the screen, so it sees your eyes from a little above or below rather than straight on, which makes matters harder still. And small head movements, such as a slight nod, can masquerade as vertical eye movements.

The result is that webcam trackers are commonly off by a few degrees. On a laptop at arm’s length, that can span several lines of text, which is a real problem for an app that needs to know when you’ve reached the last line.

## Letting reading do the work

This is where the habits described in this book come to the rescue. Gaze Reader doesn’t rely on the vertical estimate alone. It treats “which line is the reader on?” as a question of probabilities, keeping a running estimate for every visible line and updating it with each fixation. The reliable horizontal signal reveals the steady left-to-right march along a line, and each big leftward return sweep strongly suggests a move to the next line. The rough vertical estimate adds its own evidence, and the app also learns how that estimate tends to drift over time. The technical name for this kind of reasoning is a *hidden Markov model*.

When the app becomes confident that you’re on the last line of the page and your eyes reach its end, or sweep back looking for a next line that isn’t there, it turns the page. There is also a deliberate gesture, switched on by default: glance below the bottom edge of the page for a moment, and the app takes the hint.

A few habits help the tracker a great deal: light your face from the front rather than sitting with a bright window behind you, keep your head roughly where it was during calibration, and recalibrate after moving to a new chair, changing the lighting or tilting the screen. Glasses are usually fine, though strong reflections on the lenses can confuse the landmarks. Webcam eye tracking will never match a laboratory instrument, but with a little help from the structure of reading, it can be surprisingly good at knowing when you’re ready for the next page.

# Chapter 7: Rest Your Eyes

Reading asks a lot of some very small muscles. To keep close text in focus, a ring of muscle inside each eye, the *ciliary muscle*, has to stay contracted so that the lens can take on a rounder, more powerful shape. At the same time, the muscles that aim your eyes must keep them turned slightly inward so that both point at the same word. Holding that posture for a long time is tiring, in much the same way that holding a book out at arm’s length is tiring for your arms.

## Why long reads feel tiring

Long sessions of close work, especially on screens, can leave people with tired, dry or burning eyes, blurred vision, headaches and a stiff neck. Eye-care professionals often group these symptoms under the name *digital eye strain*. For most people the discomfort is temporary and eases with rest.

A big part of the story is blinking. People blink markedly less when they concentrate on reading or on a screen, often around half as often as usual, and many of the blinks they do make are incomplete. Every blink spreads a fresh layer of tears across the eye, so blinking less lets the surface dry out. If your eyes feel gritty after a long session, that is a likely reason.

## The 20-20-20 habit

A simple habit helps with all of this, and it is easy to remember: every twenty minutes, look at something about twenty feet away, roughly six meters, for about twenty seconds. Looking into the distance lets the ciliary muscle relax and lets your eyes stop converging, and the pause is a good moment to blink a few times on purpose. The 20-20-20 rule is widely recommended by eye-care professionals. It is a rule of thumb rather than a precise prescription, so don’t worry if your window only offers fifteen feet of view. What matters is the regular break.

Gaze Reader’s break reminders are switched on by default, so every twenty minutes or so Dewey will nudge you. He takes the job very seriously.

A few adjustments can make long reads more comfortable, too. Place your screen about an arm’s length away, with its top at or slightly below eye level, so that you look a little downward. Reduce glare from windows and overhead lights, and try to keep the room’s lighting roughly in balance with the screen’s brightness rather than reading a bright screen in a dark room. Make the text larger if you catch yourself leaning in. And choose whichever theme feels easiest on your eyes, whether light, sepia or dark; comfort here is personal.

## Good news for tired eyes

Reading in dim light may tire your eyes, but it won’t damage them. The blue light from screens has not been shown to harm the eye, and glasses that filter blue light have not been shown to reduce eye strain; breaks and blinking are what help. If you read late at night, though, dimming the screen and warming its colors may make it easier to wind down.

Changes with age are normal, too. Somewhere in their forties, most people notice that close focusing is getting harder, because the lens inside the eye gradually loses its flexibility. This is called *presbyopia*, and it is why so many people start holding menus farther away. Reading glasses, larger text and good light all help. For children, more time spent outdoors has been linked to a lower risk of becoming nearsighted, which is a fine excuse to take a book outside.

If discomfort persists, or you notice changes in your vision, see an optometrist or an ophthalmologist. An uncorrected vision problem is a common and very fixable cause of eye strain.

## The last line

Over these seven chapters, your eyes have made thousands of saccades, skipped a good share of the words, glanced back now and then to double-check, and swept across the page at the end of every line, all without your noticing a thing. They have been doing this, quietly and brilliantly, for as long as you have been reading.

So here is a small thank-you. Look up from the page, find something far away, and give your eyes twenty seconds of rest. They’ve earned it.
