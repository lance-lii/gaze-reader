# Chapter 1: Closing Time

Iris Calloway had not slept properly in nineteen days. She knew it was nineteen because she had counted, lying awake, and she suspected the counting was part of the problem.

It wasn’t anything dramatic. Nobody was ill; nothing had burned down. It was only that, somewhere along the way, her life had filled up with unfinished things, and at night they came and sat on the end of the bed like cats that wanted feeding. There was a sweater with one sleeve. There was a letter to her sister Clem that began *Dear Clem, I’m sorry about* and then stopped, because Iris could never decide what she was sorry about first. And there was a library book called *The Seven Winters of Odile Marchbank*, which she had renewed five times and had been reading, in the sense of carrying it around, for months.

She had been stuck on page 187 for a month. She would read the first paragraph, then read it again, then realize she had read it four times and could not say what it was about, and then it would be three in the morning and she would lay the book face down on her chest like a small roof and stare at the ceiling.

On Thursday the library emailed her. The book was due tomorrow. It could not be renewed again. Someone had placed a hold on it.

So on Thursday evening, after work, Iris took the book to the Larkin Street Library, because the library was the only place she knew where the quiet came in exactly the right size.

The reading room was on the second floor, up a staircase with a brass rail worn pale by a century of hands. It had tall windows, long oak tables, green-shaded lamps and one enormous armchair in the corner, upholstered in green velvet gone soft and bald at the arms. Beside it stood an iron radiator that ticked and clanked as though someone deep inside the building were politely tapping on the pipes with a spoon. The whole room smelled of old paper, floor polish and, faintly, of vanilla.

Iris sank into the armchair and opened the book to page 187. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, a clock measured out the evening in patient, wooden seconds.

*On the first night of the fourth winter*, she read, and then she read it again, and then, for the first time in nineteen days, Iris Calloway fell asleep.

When she woke, the lamps were out.

The windows had gone the deep, bruised blue of streetlight on a cloudy night. The long tables were shadows. Downstairs, the great clock in the lobby cleared its throat and began, very slowly, to strike. Iris counted the chimes the way she counted everything. There were twelve.

She sat up so quickly that the book slid off her lap. She hurried downstairs to the front doors, which were locked with the kind of lock that wanted a key she didn’t have. A red sign beside the side door said EMERGENCY EXIT ONLY. ALARM WILL SOUND.

She took out her phone. The screen stayed black.

“Of course,” Iris said to the phone, which did not argue.

She stood in the dark lobby for a while, considering her options, which were few, and her dignity, which was fewer. Then she climbed back up to the reading room, because at least the armchair was warm.

She had just picked up the book when a small voice spoke from somewhere low on the nearest shelf.

“Oh,” it said. “Oh, dear. You’re not supposed to be here.”

There was a pause.

“I mean, hello. Good evening. Welcome to the library.” Another pause. “You’re still not supposed to be here.”

# Chapter 2: A Resident of Shelf 020

There was a click, and a tiny beam of light appeared, about as bright as a firefly with ambitions. It found Iris’s shoes, lingered on them as if embarrassed, and climbed politely up to her face.

Behind it, standing on the third shelf of the nonfiction section, was a person roughly the height of a paperback book.

He had a great deal of hair, most of it going in the same direction, except for a single cowlick at the crown that stood straight up like an exclamation point that had lost its sentence. His glasses were enormous and perfectly round, and the glow of his little light glinted off them in two bright moons. He had freckles, a bow tie that was very slightly crooked, an argyle sweater vest and, in the breast pocket of his shirt, a plastic pocket protector with room for three pens. Two were in it. The third was in his hand, and it was a flashlight. Under his other arm he held a book the size of a postage stamp.

“I’m Dewey,” he said. “Like the decimal system.” He pushed his glasses up his nose. “No relation. Well. Some relation. We have a lot in common. We both like things to be where they belong.”

“I’m Iris.”

Dewey’s eyes went wide behind his lenses. “Like the eye!”

“Like the flower, usually.”

“Like the eye,” Dewey said firmly. “The colored ring around the pupil. It has two little muscles in it, did you know? One makes the pupil smaller when it’s bright and one makes it bigger when it’s dark. Your pupils must be wide open right now. It’s extremely dark in here.” He seemed to realize something. “Sorry. I’m a bit of a nerd. It’s in my job description. I wrote my job description.”

“You live here?”

“On 020.” He gestured grandly at the shelf beneath his feet. “Library and information science. It seemed only polite.”

Iris sat down in the green armchair, because her knees suggested it. “Dewey, I’m locked in.”

“Yes,” said Dewey, with great sympathy. “The doors are locked from nine at night until Ms. Halloran comes in at eight. The side door will open, but it sets off the alarm, and the alarm is extremely loud and brings two fire engines and a very disappointed man named Gary.”

“And my phone is dead.”

“Is anyone waiting for you at home?”

Iris thought about it. “A fern,” she said. “It has low expectations.”

“Then I suggest,” said Dewey, “that you stay. I can show you where the good blanket is.”

It was, Iris had to admit, the most reasonable suggestion anyone had made to her in weeks. She didn’t expect to sleep again; she almost never managed it twice in one night. She said so, and then, because it was dark and he was very small and the dark makes confessions easier, she told him about the other things, too. The sweater with one sleeve. The letter to Clem. The nineteen nights.

Dewey listened with his whole face. When she had finished, he took the red pen out of his pocket protector, produced a notebook from somewhere and said, “Shall we make a list?”

“I have a list,” said Iris. “The list is the problem.”

“Ah.” He put the pen away. Then his light fell on the book in her lap, and his face lit up so brightly that he hardly needed the flashlight.

“Oh! *The Seven Winters of Odile Marchbank!* That’s a wonderful—” He clapped a hand over his mouth. “I can’t say. I never say. No spoilers. It’s in the oath.”

“There’s an oath?”

“I wrote it myself,” said Dewey. “It’s very strict.”

He hopped down onto the arm of the chair, peered at the due-date slip and did a quick sum. “Due today. Technically. It’s after midnight.”

“Someone’s put a hold on it.”

“Then we have a deadline.” He straightened his bow tie, which made it more crooked. “What page are you on?”

“One eighty-seven. Out of three hundred and four.”

“One hundred and seventeen pages,” Dewey breathed, with the joy of someone who has just been handed a lovely puzzle. “Between now and eight o’clock. That’s about one page every four minutes. We could practically *stroll*.” He clasped his hands. “Iris, would you like to finish a book tonight?”

Iris looked at the book, and at the dark windows, and at the very small person in the argyle vest.

“Yes,” she said, and was surprised to find that she meant it.

# Chapter 3: The Little Leap

The good blanket turned out to be a tartan one from the lost-and-found box, with a paper tag pinned to one corner that said LOST: MARCH. Dewey was apologetic about the tag. “Nobody ever came for it,” he said. “I like to think it found us instead.”

Then he directed Iris downstairs to the staff room, which had a kettle, a box of teabags and a mug that said WORLD’S OKAYEST LIBRARIAN. He rode on her shoulder, holding on to her collar, and navigated. “Left. Left again. Mind the step stool; it bites.” At the tea shelf he leaned out and pointed. “Chamomile. No caffeine. I’ve checked the box twice.”

On the way back up, Iris sniffed the air. “Why does this whole building smell like vanilla?”

“Old paper,” said Dewey happily. “As it breaks down, it gives off a compound that smells a bit like vanilla. Technically, the books are very, very slowly turning into dessert.”

In the reading room, Iris switched on one green lamp, which made a small warm room inside the big dark one. She tucked herself into the armchair under LOST: MARCH with the tea at her elbow. Dewey slid down her sleeve to the broad arm of the chair, sat with his legs dangling, and opened his tiny book on his knees as though he, too, were settling in to read.

Iris opened *The Seven Winters of Odile Marchbank* to page 187.

> On the first night of the fourth winter, the fog came in so thick that Odile could not see the bottom of her own staircase, and Barnacle, who was old and opinionated, refused to go down it on principle.

She read on. Odile was the keeper of a lighthouse on a small gray island. Barnacle was her dog, a shaggy, elderly animal of no particular breed who disapproved of weather, visitors and change, in that order. Iris had forgotten how much she liked them both.

After a while she noticed that Dewey was not looking at his book. He was watching her.

“Are you reading,” Iris asked, “or watching me read?”

“Both,” said Dewey. “Mostly watching. I like to watch the jumps.”

“The what?”

“Your eyes. Everyone thinks their eyes slide along the line like a bead on a string, but they don’t. They hop. They stop on a word, then jump, then stop, then jump, three or four times every second. The stops are called fixations and the jumps are called saccades.” He said *saccades* the way some people say *chocolate*. “And at the end of every line, your eyes make one great big leap back to the start of the next. That’s called the return sweep. It’s my favorite.”

“You have a favorite eye movement.”

“Doesn’t everyone?” He pushed his glasses up. “Here’s the best part. At the bottom of the page—the right-hand page; I’m not an amateur—your eyes do the return sweep, and there’s no next line. So they go looking for it. That’s how I know.”

“Know what?”

“When to do this.”

He stood, took hold of the corner of the page with both arms, and heaved. Relative to Dewey, the page was roughly the size of a bedsheet, and turning it involved a great deal of effort, some staggering and one small noise of triumph. The page flopped over.

“You don’t have to—”

“I *like* to,” said Dewey, slightly out of breath. “Go on.”

She went on. And it was strange, but easier. Perhaps it was the lamp, or the tea, or the fact that someone was paying such delighted attention. Odile trimmed the wick and wrote in the logbook. Barnacle ate a sock. The fog lifted and came back. Every time Iris reached the bottom of a right-hand page and her eyes went hunting for a line that wasn’t there, the page turned, with a grunt and a flourish.

Once, she stopped.

“I keep going back,” she said. “I read a line and my eyes jump back to the one before. That’s why I never get anywhere.”

“Everyone’s eyes go back,” said Dewey at once. “Roughly one jump in every ten, for grown-up readers. They’re called regressions, which is a terrible name for a very sensible thing. It isn’t going backward. It’s checking your work.”

“Is there anything you don’t know about eyes?”

“Lots,” said Dewey. “For instance, I don’t know why they leak at the sad parts of books. I’ve read all the theories. None of them is satisfying.”

They read. The clock downstairs struck one, and later two. Page 200 went by, and 205, and 210. Then Dewey heaved over page 211, and Iris looked down at page 212 and stopped.

At the top of it, in small, stern capitals, it said: CHAPTER NINETEEN. IN WHICH BARNACLE GOES OUT INTO THE STORM.

Iris closed the book.

“No,” she said.

# Chapter 4: In Which Barnacle Goes Out Into the Storm

“The dog dies,” said Iris. “I know he dies. I flipped ahead weeks ago and saw that title, and I put the book down, and I haven’t been able to pick it up properly since. Nobody who goes out into the storm in chapter nineteen comes back in chapter twenty.”

Dewey opened his mouth. Then he closed it. Then he opened it again and said, in a strangled voice, “I can’t tell you.”

“I know. The oath.”

“The oath,” Dewey agreed miserably. He sat down on the arm of the chair and hugged his knees. For a while neither of them said anything. The radiator ticked.

“It isn’t really the dog,” said Iris at last.

Dewey waited. He was very good at waiting. It turned out to be the thing Iris most needed.

“Clem and I had a fight,” she said. “In the summer. It was about something stupid, a visit that got cancelled, and then it was about everything, the way fights are. And I started that letter, and I got as far as *I’m sorry about*, and I couldn’t think how to finish it, because what if I finish it wrong? What if I send it and she doesn’t write back? As long as it isn’t finished, it can’t go wrong.” She looked at the closed book. “I think I’ve been doing that with everything.”

Dewey pushed up his glasses, thought for a moment, and pushed them up again, which is what he did when a thought was important.

“Here is a thing I know about books,” he said. “The ending is already written. It’s sitting there on page three hundred and four, being whatever it is, whether you read it or not. You can’t make it go wrong by reading it. The only thing you get to choose is whether you’re there when it happens.”

Iris said nothing.

“Also,” Dewey added, “chapter nineteen is only twelve pages long. I counted. A hard chapter is only as long as it is. And you won’t be by yourself. I’ll be right here on the arm of the chair.” He drew himself up to his full height, which was not very full. “I’m very small, but I’m *extremely* present.”

Iris laughed despite herself. It came out wobbly.

“Here’s an idea,” said Dewey. “Read it out loud. Quietly. Reading aloud is slower than reading in your head, which is nice when you’re nervous. You can’t rush past anything, and nothing can sneak up on you.”

So Iris opened the book to page 212, took a breath and began to read chapter nineteen out loud, softly, to a library full of sleeping books and one extremely present nerd.

> The wind took the door out of her hand, and the dark took the dog, and Odile stood on the step shouting his name into a night that had no interest in names.

Odile searched the rocks, the boathouse, the empty coal shed and the long wet grass where Barnacle liked to lie in summer. She called until her voice was gone. Dewey listened with both hands clamped over his bow tie.

Halfway down page 218, just as Odile’s lantern blew out, the radiator beside them gave a tremendous BANG, like a giant dropping a saucepan.

Iris shrieked. Dewey fell off the arm of the chair and landed in the blanket.

“That,” said Dewey from somewhere inside LOST: MARCH, in a voice of great dignity, “was water hammer. Steam and water knocking about in the pipes. Perfectly harmless.” He climbed back up, straightening his glasses. “I nearly died.”

Iris was laughing too hard to read, and then she wasn’t, and then she was reading again, faster now, her voice barely above a whisper. Odile came back to the lighthouse alone as the storm blew itself out. She did not light the fire. She sat in her wet coat at the kitchen table, and the chapter ended with a single line.

> In the morning, the sea was flat and bright and innocent, as if it had never done anything wrong in its life.

Iris’s eyes reached the end of the line, swept back to the left and found nothing there. She could not breathe.

Dewey took hold of the page and turned it.

# Chapter 5: The Warmest Place

Chapter twenty began on page 224. Iris read it silently, because she could not have read it aloud if she tried.

Odile climbed the hundred and twelve steps of the lighthouse at dawn, as she had every morning for thirty-one years, to put out the great lamp. She climbed slowly. She did not want to reach the top, because at the top there would be the view, and in the view there would be the whole island, and somewhere on the island there would be—

> He was lying against the warm brass base of the lamp, curled into a shape like a comma, snoring. He had been there all night. He had come in through the coal door when the wind turned, climbed the hundred and twelve steps on his old legs, and gone to the one warm place in the whole cold tower, and he had slept through every minute of her fear.

Iris made a noise that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob and was mostly both. Then her eyes filled up and overflowed.

Dewey was already holding up a tissue with both arms. He had pulled it from a box larger than himself, and it had come out in one long, billowing piece, like a magician’s scarf.

“Why,” said Iris, taking it, “are my eyes leaking? The dog *lived*.”

“I told you,” said Dewey kindly. “Nobody’s completely sure. Emotional tears are one of the great unsolved mysteries.” He considered. “Personally, I think it’s your eyes applauding.”

Iris blew her nose. Then she looked at him narrowly.

“You’d read it,” she said. “You knew all along.”

“I have read it seven times,” said Dewey, with enormous dignity. “I did not *spoil* it. Those are different things. It’s in the oath.”

After that, the book seemed to open up in front of her like a road.

Iris read, and Dewey turned, and the pages went by in a steady, easy rhythm. She wasn’t trying to go faster. She simply wanted to know what happened, and wanting to know, it turned out, made the words come quickly. Dewey hardly needed to watch her eyes anymore. He knew the rhythm of her reading now, the way you know the footsteps of someone you live with, and he was ready at the bottom of every page with his arms around its corner.

The clock struck three, and later four. Odile kept the light for three more winters, each one shorter than the last. Barnacle grew grayer and more opinionated. A young woman came out on the supply boat to learn the lamp, and Barnacle disapproved of her thoroughly and then followed her everywhere. On page 301, Odile handed the young woman the keys.

On page 303, Odile and Barnacle stood at the rail of the ferry with the island behind them.

And on page 304, near the top, there was one last paragraph.

> Behind them, across the gray water, a light came on in the tower. Someone new had climbed the hundred and twelve steps. Odile did not look back; she did not need to. She knew exactly how it looked from out here: a light, left burning, for whoever came next.

Iris read the last line. Her eyes reached the end of it, swept back to the left and went looking for the next line.

There wasn’t one.

She looked up. Dewey was standing at the corner of the page with his arms at his sides. He wasn’t turning it.

“That’s the one page I never turn,” he said softly. “The last one. That one’s yours.”

Iris sat very still for a while. Then she closed the book, gently, the way you close the door of a room where someone is sleeping.

“I finished it,” she said.

“You finished it,” said Dewey, and his glasses shone.

# Chapter 6: Whoever Comes Next

For a long time, neither of them said anything. It was the good kind of silence, the kind that comes after the last page, when a story is still settling into place inside you like snow.

“How do you feel?” Dewey asked eventually.

Iris thought about it. “Finished,” she said. “In a good way. I’d forgotten there was a good way.”

She looked across the dark reading room. Along the far wall stood the old wooden card catalog, a great honey-colored cabinet of tiny drawers that nobody had used for looking things up in years. Its top drawer, which never quite closed, held blank index cards.

Dewey followed her gaze. Without a word, he climbed down the chair, crossed the floor, scaled the cabinet using the drawer handles as a ladder, and came back some minutes later, rather out of breath, dragging a blank card behind him like a sled. Then he took the blue pen from his pocket protector and held it out to her.

“The red one’s for corrections,” he explained. “The blue one’s for everything else.”

“And the third one?”

“The third one’s a flashlight,” said Dewey. “Keep up.”

Iris rested the card on the back cover of *The Seven Winters of Odile Marchbank* and looked at it for a long time. Then she wrote:

*Dear Clem, I’m sorry about the phone call, and the summer, and being stubborn, which I know is my worst quality and possibly my only hobby. I miss you. I finished a book tonight. The dog lives. Call me? Love, Iris.*

She read it over. It was not perfect. It was finished. Those, she was beginning to understand, were different things, too.

“The dog lives?” said Dewey, reading upside down.

“She’ll want to know,” said Iris.

She meant to stay awake and watch the windows turn gray. She meant to ask Dewey how long he had lived on 020, and whether he was ever lonely. But sleep came in the way the fog came in the book: all at once, and from every direction. The last things she remembered were the blanket being tugged up to her chin by someone very small, the green lamp clicking off, and a voice saying, “Good night, Iris,” as though it had been waiting all evening to say it.

---

Ms. Halloran unlocked the front doors of the Larkin Street Library at five minutes to eight, carrying a coffee and humming. Upstairs she found Iris asleep in the green armchair under a tartan blanket that said LOST: MARCH, with a finished library book in her lap, an index card addressed to someone called Clem propped against the lamp, and an empty mug on the table that said WORLD’S OKAYEST LIBRARIAN.

Iris woke, saw her and began to explain everything at once: the armchair, the clock, the doors, the phone, the fern.

Ms. Halloran only smiled. “It happens more often than you’d think,” she said. “That chair has a reputation.” She picked up the book and turned it over. “And this is due today. Right on time. Good thing, too. There’s a boy who’s been asking about this one every single day.”

At nine o’clock, as Iris was buttoning her coat to leave, he arrived: a boy of about twelve with a backpack bigger than his torso, who had clearly run the whole way. Ms. Halloran handed him the book, and he held it the way you hold something you have been waiting a long time for.

“Is it good?” he asked Iris, since she was standing there.

“It’s wonderful,” said Iris.

The boy hesitated. “Does the dog—”

“I couldn’t possibly say,” said Iris gravely. “It’s in the oath.”

---

Iris walked home through a bright, cold morning. On the way, she bought a stamp and mailed the card to Clem. She watered the fern, which had not expected her back so soon and seemed to perk up out of sheer surprise. Then she went to bed in the middle of the day and slept for nine hours without counting anything at all.

Clem called that evening. The first thing she said was, “What do you mean, *the dog lives*?”

They talked for two hours.

As for Dewey, he still lives at the Larkin Street Library, on the third shelf of the nonfiction section, between the bibliographies and the encyclopedias. But he gets around. He is fond of corners, and good lamps, and anyone who is having trouble getting to the end of something.

So if you ever find yourself awake in the small hours with a book you can’t quite finish, look for him in the corner of the page. He’ll be the one in the argyle vest, pushing his glasses up his nose and reading along with you. And when your eyes reach the last line and go looking for the next one, he’ll turn the page.

Every page, that is, except the very last one.

That one’s yours.
